# Narrative, fantasy, and surreal scenes

## Decisions

Choose the depicted instant and what it lets the viewer infer. Establish actors, relationships, setting, action, stakes, and which information can remain ambiguous. One still image cannot literally show every stage of a long event without a sequence or deliberate composite.

For fantasy, establish a few shared rules of scale, materials, technology, or magic. For surreal work, identify the intended impossibility and the surrounding stable logic. Do not add generic glowing runes, mist, castles, or floating objects because a brief says 'fantasy.'

## Visible description

Use gesture, direction, distance, props, and traces of prior action to communicate a moment. Organize foreground/middle/background only if that serves the medium. Let secondary clues support the central event rather than compete with it.

A believable imagined object needs recognizable relationships even when its purpose is fictional. Define what joins, supports, opens, or transforms if visible. In surrealism, a scale reversal or impossible material may be sufficient; multiplying anomalies can dilute the main idea.

Preserve the user's specified emotional uncertainty. A character's hesitation can be visible without inventing biography, dialogue, or a moral lesson.

## Conflicts and review

Check contradictory event timing, unclear agents, props that change ownership without reason, and effects masking the action. If several beats must be shown, discuss separate images or a requested multi-panel format rather than adding panels silently.

Do not correct deliberate impossibility as a physics error. Conversely, do not excuse accidental anatomy or incoherent perspective as surreal unless it follows the agreed design.

## Ask or research when

Ask which instant or relationship matters most when a story has competing focal events. Research a named myth, historical setting, or cultural symbol when fidelity is requested. Original fantasy need not receive invented historical authority.
