# Diagrams, information graphics, and interface previews

## Decisions

Identify the audience, learning/action goal, real content, and representation: process, comparison, chart, anatomical explanation, map, or screen. Decide what must be understood in one glance and what can be secondary. Separate decorative imagery from information-bearing marks.

## Visible description

For diagrams, name entities and relationships before drawing instructions. Distinguish flow, sequence, containment, association, and quantity. Arrows need an unambiguous meaning. Use consistent symbols, clear label anchoring, and enough separation to avoid false connections.

For charts, obtain data, units, categories, axes, and required annotations. If unavailable, ask or offer an explicitly illustrative design outside the factual claim. Never fabricate plausible numbers or citations and present them as real. Do not imply that visual generation guarantees accurate bar lengths or scales.

For interfaces, describe one coherent screen state with actual controls, content hierarchy, navigation, spacing, and readable labels. Avoid a collage of incompatible states or decorative 'futuristic UI' conventions when the request is a usable product preview. The deliverable is an image, not functioning software.

## Conflicts and review

Check reading order, arrow endpoints, legends, units, copy, group boundaries, data consistency, and text size. Scientific fidelity can require simpler imagery rather than extra detail. A beautiful diagram with wrong relationships fails its purpose.

If the content will not fit legibly, prioritize with the user or offer a requested sequence; do not shrink every label indefinitely. For mathematically exact or editable output, explain the limitation and appropriate separate production step without changing the requested deliverable unilaterally.

## Ask or research when

Ask for missing factual inputs that determine meaning. Research technical/scientific relationships using authoritative sources when uncertain. Use the nature reference for biological appearance, but verify explanatory relationships independently. Do not invent regulatory labels or unsupported claims in interface or product diagrams.
