# Nature, animals, and microscopic subjects

## Decisions

Set observation scale: landscape, habitat, organism, macro detail, or microscopic explanation. Decide between naturalistic observation, expressive interpretation, and educational representation. Identify species or environment only as precisely as the evidence and user require.

## Visible description

Organize terrain through landform, drainage, ground cover, atmospheric depth, and weather. Vegetation has growth structure, clusters, seasonal state, and scale; avoid interchangeable green texture. Place plants and animals in coherent surroundings for a naturalistic scene.

For animals, resolve body plan, species-specific visible features, pose, support, action, and interaction before fur/feather detail. Fur direction or feather layering should follow form where visible. Do not humanize expression or posture automatically. Character illustration may do so deliberately with stable rules.

For water, snow, mist, or weather, specify where it occurs and what it obscures or reveals. Coordinate light and visibility rather than adding every atmospheric effect.

At microscopic scale, define whether the image represents an observation, false-color interpretation, or explanatory diagram. Do not imply that an illustrative color scheme or imagined structure is direct scientific evidence.

## Conflicts and review

Check body-part counts, incompatible habitat/season cues, vegetation growing through impossible supports, floating contact points, and macro-level detail imposed on distant subjects. Distinguish observation from artistic license. Patterns can be simplified intentionally without being called scientifically exact.

## Ask or research when

Research species anatomy, ecology, geological processes, or cellular structures when accuracy is central or knowledge is uncertain. Ask for taxonomic specificity only if it changes the result. For educational relationships and labels, also use the diagrams reference; never invent a scale bar without a supported scale.
