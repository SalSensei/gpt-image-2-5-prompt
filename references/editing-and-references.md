# References, edits, and result-led revision

Use for supplied images, reference-guided creation, and edits. Distinguish creating a new composition using references from changing a particular existing image.

## Inventory and interpretation

Inspect available images rather than inferring their contents from filenames. Record actual order or existing labels, intended role, adopted attributes, and boundaries. User assignments take precedence over your interpretation. Possible roles include identity, garment, object, gesture, spatial layout, palette, medium, and edit original; one image can supply several explicitly bounded roles.

Do not force incidental objects or every supplied reference into the work. If references disagree, identify which controls the disputed attribute. An identity image need not control outfit or background. A mood reference need not supply its person or text. Distinguish observed appearance from unsupported claims about identity, provenance, or exact material composition.

For an inaccessible essential original, ask for access or reattachment through the host's supported interaction. Continue independent choices, but do not invent the image or call an unresolved template ready to submit. For optional missing references, describe what can be completed from user text and avoid nonexistent image numbers. Preserve established labels rather than renumbering silently.

## Local edit specification

Identify the original, target object/region, requested change, necessary local consequences, and invariants. For a garment replacement, body and identity may stay fixed while fabric contact shadows must adapt. Do not demand unchanged pixels precisely where a physical change requires new occlusion or illumination.

For complex edits, make invariants concrete: pose, proportions, camera placement, framing, background layout, neighboring text, and other relevant attributes. Avoid an indiscriminate preservation list that conflicts with the requested change. A removed object may reveal previously hidden content; decide whether a neutral plausible fill suffices or whether the user must specify it.

When exact untouched pixels matter, distinguish the editable region from the protected original. Explain that a compositing workflow is needed; this prompt-writing skill does not perform that operation or promise prompting alone will enforce it.

## Integration and style transfer

When inserting an element, align its scale, contact, perspective, light, and visual medium with the destination unless contrast is deliberate. Specify location relative to recognizable scene elements instead of ambiguous 'over there.' For style transfer, separate palette, marks, texture, and medium from source subject matter.

For identity-sensitive changes, prioritize features that define the particular subject. Do not invent a new idealized face or proportions under 'beautification.' A product label can be more important than background polish. Preserve exact requested copy and check any edit that could disturb it.

## Iteration

Use the accepted result as the next input and name it unambiguously in sequential instructions. If it is not available yet, explain that dependency outside the prompt. Avoid a series of independent generations described as exact edits.

With a result in view, list the observed mismatch, its consequence, a possible cause if useful, and a focused correction. Hypotheses are not observations. Check collateral changes after a revision. If repeated edits erode approved details, consider restarting from the best original with consolidated instructions rather than accumulating repairs; discuss the tradeoff when it affects accepted work.

Keep separate complete prompts for separate images or explicitly requested edit steps. Do not fragment one image's overlong specification to evade the character ceiling.
