# Medium and making

Read relevant sections; combine with the applicable domain. Medium is not an obligatory texture overlay.

## Photography

Resolve viewpoint, distance, field of view, crop, focus distribution, and motion before equipment names. Translate equipment cues into visible intent. Close wide-angle proximity and distant compressed views organize faces and architecture differently; do not demand contradictory appearances.

Choose source direction, relative size, and relationship to ambient/fill light. State what light reveals: relief, translucency, gloss, gesture, separation. Add catchlights, reflected color, and shadow softness where they matter. Align the surroundings with the proposed illumination.

Separate tonal treatment from mood: retained highlight texture, deep or open shadows, restrained or saturated color, arrested motion or controlled blur. Grain, bloom, shallow focus, rim light, and retouching are optional. Do not automatically combine documentary restraint with polished advertising effects.

## Painting and illustration

Define construction through contour, flat masses, tonal modeling, transparent layers, opaque strokes, or marks. Balance observation and stylization. Drawing economy and edge hierarchy may matter more than naming a movement.

For watercolor, choose relevant reserved paper, wash behavior, wet transitions, or dry marks; not every effect is necessary. For opaque paint, consider coverage, underlayers, relief, and stroke scale. Vector-like illustration needs clear shapes and simplification; raster output is not an editable vector file.

Do not add microscopic skin texture to a simplified figure. Establish deliberate deformation rather than repairing every anatomical departure. In hybrids, assign each visual language a role.

## Three-dimensional and digital form

Resolve geometry and silhouette before surface detail. Decide whether objects feel manufactured, sculpted, toy-like, architectural, or physically plausible. Differentiate matte, glossy, metallic, translucent, and emissive responses rather than giving everything the same sheen.

Match texture scale to object size; distinguish relief from printed decoration. Attend to edge treatment and contact. Choose flat shading, miniature-set appearance, product visualization, or realistic space intentionally. Rendering jargon is not a mandatory quality bundle; describe necessary effects.

## Printmaking and printing

Tie cut marks, etched lines, screen shapes, ink coverage, separations, and paper exposure to the chosen process. Roughness may be structural rather than a distressed filter.

Distinguish flat artwork from a photograph of a physical print; only the latter necessarily involves physical presentation lighting. Registration offset, spread, grain, wear, and limited inks are choices. Print language does not require antique yellow paper.

## Collage, paper, textile, and mixed media

Define fragment relationships, cut/torn edges, layer order, thickness, attachment, and relief shadows. Where relevant and known, distinguish woven, stitched, felted, knitted, and printed structures. Keep fiber scale credible.

Assign layers in mixed work: painted landscape behind paper figures, or photographic fragments within a graphic grid. Decide how scale, lighting, and color unify, or where discontinuity is intentional. Do not request every medium on every surface.

## Review and research

Check that detailed clauses reinforce the selected process. For uncertain craft-specific claims, use a reliable technical or museum source or clarify fidelity versus interpretation. A generated concept is not automatically a fabrication-ready asset.
